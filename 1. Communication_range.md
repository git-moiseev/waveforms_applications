# Communication Range from Transmitter and Receiver Parameters

## Scope

The acquisition strategy developed in *Blind Acquisition* determines how much
signal energy must be collected before a receiver can distinguish a beacon
candidate from thermal noise while searching over an unknown signal state. This
section connects that acquisition requirement to physical transmitter and
receiver parameters. The result is a link equation for the maximum free-space
distance as a function of transmitted power, antenna dimensions, wavelength,
system noise temperature, integration time, propagation losses, and the size of
the blind search.

The derivation applies to a continuously radiated narrowband waveform. It is
therefore more naturally expressed in terms of accumulated signal energy than
in terms of the instantaneous signal-to-noise ratio in an arbitrarily selected
receiver bandwidth.

## Received Power

Let

- $P_t$ be the total RF power delivered to the transmitting antenna;
- $G_t$ and $G_r$ be the transmitter and receiver antenna gains in the
  direction of the link;
- $R$ be the propagation distance;
- $\lambda=c/f_0$ be the wavelength at the nominal carrier frequency;
- $L\leq 1$ be the product of all additional power-transfer factors.

The factor $L$ may include pointing loss, polarization mismatch, atmospheric
or interstellar attenuation, implementation loss, and any other loss not
already included in the antenna gains. In the far field, the Friis equation
gives

$$
P_r = P_t G_t G_r \left(\frac{\lambda}{4\pi R}\right)^2 L . \qquad (1)
$$

For aperture antennas,

$$
G=\frac{4\pi A_{\mathrm{eff}}}{\lambda^2}. \qquad (2)
$$

where $A_{\mathrm{eff}}$ is the effective collecting area. Substitution into
Eq. (1) gives the particularly useful aperture form

$$
P_r = \frac{P_t A_{\mathrm{eff},t}A_{\mathrm{eff},r}}{\lambda^2R^2}L . \qquad (3)
$$

For circular aperture antennas of physical diameters $D_t$ and $D_r$,

$$
A_{\mathrm{eff},t} = \eta_t\frac{\pi D_t^2}{4}, \qquad A_{\mathrm{eff},r} = \eta_r\frac{\pi D_r^2}{4}. \qquad (4)
$$

where $\eta_t$ and $\eta_r$ are aperture efficiencies. Equation (3) then
becomes

$$
P_r = \frac{P_t\,\eta_t\eta_r\pi^2D_t^2D_r^2}{16\lambda^2R^2}L . \qquad (5)
$$

## Receiver Noise Temperature

The one-sided thermal-noise power spectral density referred to the receiver
input is

$$
N_0=k_{\mathrm B}T_{\mathrm{sys}}. \qquad (6)
$$

where $k_{\mathrm B}$ is Boltzmann's constant and $T_{\mathrm{sys}}$ is the
system noise temperature. A useful decomposition is

$$
T_{\mathrm{sys}} = T_{\mathrm{sky}} + T_{\mathrm{atm}} + T_{\mathrm{ground}} + T_{\mathrm{spill}} + T_{\mathrm{rx}}. \qquad (7)
$$

The receiver contribution can be obtained from its noise figure $F$:

$$
T_{\mathrm{rx}}=T_0(F-1). \qquad (8)
$$

with $T_0=290\ \mathrm{K}$. Loss ahead of the first low-noise amplifier is
especially expensive. If a passive input network has a linear loss
$\ell\geq1$, physical temperature $T_p$, and is followed by a receiver with
noise temperature $T_{\mathrm{rx}}$, its input-referred contribution is

$$
T_{\mathrm{front}} = (\ell-1)T_p+\ell T_{\mathrm{rx}}. \qquad (9)
$$

Thus, increasing antenna area, reducing preamplifier loss, and reducing system
temperature enter the range equation on an equal physical footing.

## Energy Required for Acquisition

Let $T_{\mathrm{int}}$ be the coherent integration time for one tested signal
hypothesis. During this interval, the received signal energy is

$$
E_s=P_rT_{\mathrm{int}}. \qquad (10)
$$

Define the required normalized acquisition energy

$$
\rho_{\mathrm{acq}} \equiv \frac{E_s}{N_0} = \frac{P_rT_{\mathrm{int}}}{k_{\mathrm B}T_{\mathrm{sys}}}. \qquad (11)
$$

Unlike a conventional link margin stated as $P_r/(kT_{\mathrm{sys}}B)$,
Eq. (11) does not depend on an arbitrary analysis bandwidth. For an ideal
coherent observation, the effective noise bandwidth is of order
$1/T_{\mathrm{int}}$, so the bandwidth and integration-time descriptions are
equivalent.

The value of $\rho_{\mathrm{acq}}$ is not universal. It is fixed by the
required probabilities of detection and false alarm and by the number of
independent hypotheses tested during blind acquisition. If $M$ independent
noise cells are searched and the allowed experiment-wide false-alarm
probability is $P_{\mathrm{FA}}$, the single-cell false-alarm probability must
be approximately

$$
p_{\mathrm{fa}}\simeq\frac{P_{\mathrm{FA}}}{M}. \qquad (12)
$$

For a normalized exponential noise-energy statistic, the corresponding
noise-only threshold is

$$
\gamma = -\ln p_{\mathrm{fa}} \simeq \ln\left(\frac{M}{P_{\mathrm{FA}}}\right). \qquad (13)
$$

The exact required $\rho_{\mathrm{acq}}$ must be obtained from the chosen
detection statistic and target probability $P_D$; Eq. (13) shows the
important scaling:

$$
\rho_{\mathrm{acq}} \sim \ln\left(\frac{M}{P_{\mathrm{FA}}}\right) + \text{detection margin}. \qquad (14)
$$

If the uncertainty interval in frequency is $W_f$, the number of independent
frequency cells is approximately

$$
M_f\simeq W_fT_{\mathrm{int}}. \qquad (15)
$$

Searches over frequency drift, symbol period, time offset, sky position, or
waveform family multiply the total $M$. Consequently, a computationally
simple waveform improves physical range indirectly: it reduces the number of
required hypotheses and therefore reduces $\rho_{\mathrm{acq}}$. The
dependence is only logarithmic for an ideal threshold detector, but it is real
and it is absent from a conventional known-signal link budget.

For a two-frequency constant-total-power waveform, $P_t$ denotes the combined
power in both frequency states. If acquisition noncoherently combines the
energy from both states, Eq. (11) uses the total received power. If the receiver
must detect either component independently and each carries a fraction
$\alpha_i$ of the total power, replace $P_t$ by
$\alpha_{\min}P_t$, where
$\alpha_{\min}=\min_i\alpha_i$. For equal components,
$\alpha_{\min}=1/2$, producing a factor-of-two power penalty, or a
$\sqrt{2}$ reduction in range, relative to ideal combined-energy detection.

## Maximum Communication Distance

At the limiting distance, Eq. (11) is satisfied with equality:

$$
P_r = \frac{\rho_{\mathrm{acq}}k_{\mathrm B}T_{\mathrm{sys}}}{T_{\mathrm{int}}}. \qquad (16)
$$

Combining Eqs. (1) and (16) gives the gain form of the range equation:

$$
\boxed{R_{\max} = \frac{\lambda}{4\pi}\sqrt{\frac{P_tG_tG_rLT_{\mathrm{int}}}{\rho_{\mathrm{acq}}k_{\mathrm B}T_{\mathrm{sys}}}}}. \qquad (17)
$$

Using effective antenna areas gives

$$
\boxed{R_{\max} = \sqrt{\frac{P_tA_{\mathrm{eff},t}A_{\mathrm{eff},r}LT_{\mathrm{int}}}{\rho_{\mathrm{acq}}k_{\mathrm B}T_{\mathrm{sys}}\lambda^2}}}. \qquad (18)
$$

For circular apertures, the result becomes

$$
\boxed{R_{\max} = \frac{\pi D_tD_r}{4\lambda}\sqrt{\frac{P_t\eta_t\eta_rLT_{\mathrm{int}}}{\rho_{\mathrm{acq}}k_{\mathrm B}T_{\mathrm{sys}}}}}. \qquad (19)
$$

Equation (19) is the requested relation between transmitter power, antenna
dimensions, receiver temperature, observation time, wavelength, acquisition
requirements, and achievable distance.

It may also be inverted to obtain the required transmitter power:

$$
\boxed{P_t = \frac{16\rho_{\mathrm{acq}}k_{\mathrm B}T_{\mathrm{sys}}\lambda^2R^2}{\pi^2\eta_t\eta_rD_t^2D_r^2LT_{\mathrm{int}}}}. \qquad (20)
$$

Similarly, the required product of antenna diameters is

$$
\boxed{D_tD_r = \frac{4\lambda R}{\pi}\sqrt{\frac{\rho_{\mathrm{acq}}k_{\mathrm B}T_{\mathrm{sys}}}{P_t\eta_t\eta_rLT_{\mathrm{int}}}}}. \qquad (21)
$$

## Scaling Form

At the neutral-hydrogen frequency
$f_0=1.420405751\ \mathrm{GHz}$, for which
$\lambda\simeq0.2111\ \mathrm{m}$, Eq. (19) can be written as

$$
\begin{aligned} R_{\max}\simeq{}& 2.05\ \mathrm{pc}\left(\frac{P_t}{1\ \mathrm{MW}}\right)^{1/2}\left(\frac{D_t}{10\ \mathrm{m}}\right)\left(\frac{D_r}{10\ \mathrm{m}}\right) \\ &{}\times\left(\frac{\eta_t}{0.6}\right)^{1/2}\left(\frac{\eta_r}{0.6}\right)^{1/2}\left(\frac{L}{1}\right)^{1/2}\left(\frac{T_{\mathrm{int}}}{1000\ \mathrm{s}}\right)^{1/2} \\ &{}\times\left(\frac{30\ \mathrm{K}}{T_{\mathrm{sys}}}\right)^{1/2}\left(\frac{30}{\rho_{\mathrm{acq}}}\right)^{1/2}. \qquad (22) \end{aligned}
$$

This numerical example is only a normalization, not an assumed practical
design. For the same parameters, extending the distance to $5\ \mathrm{pc}$
requires approximately $5.9\ \mathrm{MW}$, because the required power grows
as $R^2$.

## Interpretation and Design Consequences

Equation (19) gives several immediate results:

1. Range is proportional to each antenna diameter:
   $R_{\max}\propto D_tD_r$. For aperture antennas, doubling either diameter
   doubles the range.
2. Range grows only as the square root of transmitted power and integration
   time:
   $R_{\max}\propto\sqrt{P_tT_{\mathrm{int}}}$.
3. Range decreases as the square root of system temperature and required
   acquisition energy:
   $R_{\max}\propto1/\sqrt{T_{\mathrm{sys}}\rho_{\mathrm{acq}}}$.
4. At fixed physical aperture dimensions, range is inversely proportional to
   wavelength, or directly proportional to frequency. This gain is limited in
   practice by surface accuracy, pointing requirements, propagation, and the
   frequency dependence of $T_{\mathrm{sys}}$.
5. A loss factor reduces range only through its square root, but avoiding loss
   before the receiver's first amplifier also prevents a simultaneous increase
   in $T_{\mathrm{sys}}$.
6. Reducing blind-search dimensionality decreases both computation and the
   multiple-trial detection threshold. Waveform design therefore affects range
   even when transmitted power, antennas, and receiver hardware remain
   unchanged.

The free-space result assumes correct pointing, far-field propagation, and a
stationary channel over $T_{\mathrm{int}}$. If phase coherence is lost after a
coherence time $T_c<T_{\mathrm{int}}$, the observation must be divided into
shorter coherent intervals and combined noncoherently. In that case the ideal
linear accumulation of Eq. (11) is no longer attained, and the required
$\rho_{\mathrm{acq}}$ must be increased according to the selected
noncoherent detector. Scintillation, unknown acceleration, oscillator
instability, and unmodelled Doppler drift can all impose such a limit.

Finally, Eqs. (17)--(22) describe acquisition range, not necessarily reliable
data-decoding range. After acquisition, the communication data rate and error
probability introduce a separate $E_b/N_0$ requirement. For a beacon intended
to be found before it is decoded, the acquisition condition is the appropriate
first constraint.
