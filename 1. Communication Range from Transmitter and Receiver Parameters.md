# Communication Range from Transmitter and Receiver Parameters

## Scope

The acquisition strategy developed in *Blind Acquisition* determines how much
signal energy must be collected before a receiver can distinguish a beacon
candidate from thermal noise while searching over an unknown signal state. This
section connects that acquisition requirement to physical transmitter and
receiver parameters. The result is a link equation for the maximum free-space
distance as a function of transmitted power, antenna dimensions, wavelength,
system noise temperature, integration time, propagation losses, and the size of
the blind search.

The derivation applies to a continuously radiated narrowband waveform. It is
therefore more naturally expressed in terms of accumulated signal energy than
in terms of the instantaneous signal-to-noise ratio in an arbitrarily selected
receiver bandwidth.

## Received Power

Let

- $P_t$ be the total RF power delivered to the transmitting antenna;
- $G_t$ and $G_r$ be the transmitter and receiver antenna gains in the
  direction of the link;
- $R$ be the propagation distance;
- $\lambda=c/f_0$ be the wavelength at the nominal carrier frequency;
- $L\leq 1$ be the product of all additional power-transfer factors.

The factor $L$ may include pointing loss, polarization mismatch, atmospheric
or interstellar attenuation, implementation loss, and any other loss not
already included in the antenna gains. In the far field, the Friis equation
gives

$$P_r = P_t G_t G_r \left(\frac{\lambda}{4\pi R}\right)^2 L . \qquad (1)$$

Equation (1) is the standard free-space transmission relation introduced by
Friis [1]. The original paper also gives the equivalent formulation in terms
of the effective areas of the transmitting and receiving antennas.

For aperture antennas,

$$G=\frac{4\pi A_{\mathrm{eff}}}{\lambda^2}. \qquad (2)$$

where $A_{\mathrm{eff}}$ is the effective collecting area. Substitution into
Eq. (1) gives the particularly useful aperture form

$$P_r = \frac{P_t A_{\mathrm{eff},t}A_{\mathrm{eff},r}}{\lambda^2R^2}L . \qquad (3)$$

The gain--effective-area relation in Eq. (2), and its use in free-space link
budgets, are also documented in the NASA/JPL *Propagation Effects Handbook for
Satellite Systems* [2].

For circular aperture antennas of physical diameters $D_t$ and $D_r$,

$$A_{\mathrm{eff},t} = \eta_t\frac{\pi D_t^2}{4}, \qquad A_{\mathrm{eff},r} = \eta_r\frac{\pi D_r^2}{4}. \qquad (4)$$

where $\eta_t$ and $\eta_r$ are aperture efficiencies. Equation (3) then
becomes

$$P_r = \frac{P_t\,\eta_t\eta_r\pi^2D_t^2D_r^2}{16\lambda^2R^2}L . \qquad (5)$$

## Receiver Noise Temperature

The one-sided thermal-noise power spectral density referred to the receiver
input is

$$N_0=k_{\mathrm B}T_{\mathrm{sys}}. \qquad (6)$$

The relation between noise power density and system noise temperature used in
Eq. (6) is the standard $N_0=kT$ representation employed in NASA/JPL
telecommunication link budgets [2, 3]. The numerical value of
$k_{\mathrm B}$ is fixed exactly in the SI and is available from NIST [4].

where $k_{\mathrm B}$ is Boltzmann's constant and $T_{\mathrm{sys}}$ is the
system noise temperature. A useful decomposition is

$$T_{\mathrm{sys}} = T_{\mathrm{sky}} + T_{\mathrm{atm}} + T_{\mathrm{ground}} + T_{\mathrm{spill}} + T_{\mathrm{rx}}. \qquad (7)$$

The receiver contribution can be obtained from its noise figure $F$:

$$T_{\mathrm{rx}}=T_0(F-1). \qquad (8)$$

with $T_0=290\ \mathrm{K}$. Loss ahead of the first low-noise amplifier is
especially expensive. If a passive input network has a linear loss
$\ell\geq1$, physical temperature $T_p$, and is followed by a receiver with
noise temperature $T_{\mathrm{rx}}$, its input-referred contribution is

$$T_{\mathrm{front}} = (\ell-1)T_p+\ell T_{\mathrm{rx}}. \qquad (9)$$

Equations (8) and (9) follow the standard equivalent-input-noise-temperature
relations. NIST gives $T_{\mathrm{rx}}=290(F-1)$ for a two-port receiver [5],
while the passive-loss transformation and the transfer of following receiver
noise through a lossy input network are derived in the JPL antenna-system
noise-temperature treatment [6].

Thus, increasing antenna area, reducing preamplifier loss, and reducing system
temperature enter the range equation on an equal physical footing.

## Energy Required for Acquisition

Let $t_{\mathrm{int}}$ be the observation time for one tested signal
hypothesis. During this interval, the received signal energy is

$$E_s=P_rt_{\mathrm{int}}. \qquad (10)$$

Define the required normalized acquisition energy

$$\rho_{\mathrm{acq}} \equiv \frac{E_s}{N_0} = \frac{P_rt_{\mathrm{int}}}{k_{\mathrm B}T_{\mathrm{sys}}}. \qquad (11)$$

The use of received signal energy divided by one-sided noise spectral density
as the bandwidth-independent detection parameter is standard in digital
communications and detection theory; the CCSDS tutorial defines the analogous
$E_b/N_0$ quantity explicitly [7]. For tone acquisition with unknown phase
and frequency, Simon presents detection performance in terms of detection
signal-to-noise ratio, probability of detection, and probability of false
alarm [8].

Unlike a conventional link margin stated as $P_r/(kT_{\mathrm{sys}}B)$,
Eq. (11) does not depend on an arbitrary analysis bandwidth. For an ideal
coherent observation, the effective noise bandwidth is of order
$1/t_{\mathrm{int}}$, so the bandwidth and integration-time descriptions are
equivalent.

The value of $\rho_{\mathrm{acq}}$ is not universal. It is fixed by the
required probabilities of detection and false alarm and by the number of
independent hypotheses tested during blind acquisition. If $M$ independent
noise cells are searched and the allowed experiment-wide false-alarm
probability is $P_{\mathrm{FA}}$, the single-cell false-alarm probability must
be approximately

$$p_{\mathrm{fa}}\simeq\frac{P_{\mathrm{FA}}}{M}. \qquad (12)$$

For a normalized exponential noise-energy statistic, the corresponding
noise-only threshold is

$$\gamma = -\ln p_{\mathrm{fa}} \simeq \ln\left(\frac{M}{P_{\mathrm{FA}}}\right). \qquad (13)$$

The exact required $\rho_{\mathrm{acq}}$ must be obtained from the chosen
detection statistic and target probability $P_D$; Eq. (13) shows the
important scaling:

$$\rho_{\mathrm{acq}} \sim \ln\left(\frac{M}{P_{\mathrm{FA}}}\right) + \text{detection margin}. \qquad (14)$$

Equations (12)--(14) are the multiple-trial specialization of a
Neyman--Pearson threshold test. Equation (13) follows directly by integrating
the unit-mean exponential noise-energy density,
$p_{\mathrm{fa}}=\exp(-\gamma)$; the general dependence of a tone detector on
$P_D$, $P_{\mathrm{FA}}$, and detection SNR is treated in [8]. The
approximation in Eq. (12) assumes rare false alarms; the exact experiment-wide
relation is $P_{\mathrm{FA}}=1-(1-p_{\mathrm{fa}})^M$.

If the uncertainty interval in frequency is $W_f$, the number of independent
frequency cells is approximately

$$M_f\simeq W_ft_{\mathrm{int}}. \qquad (15)$$

Searches over frequency drift, symbol period, time offset, sky position, or
waveform family multiply the total $M$. Consequently, a computationally
simple waveform improves physical range indirectly: it reduces the number of
required hypotheses and therefore reduces $\rho_{\mathrm{acq}}$. The
dependence is only logarithmic for an ideal threshold detector, but it is real
and it is absent from a conventional known-signal link budget.

For a two-frequency constant-total-power waveform, $P_t$ denotes the combined
power in both frequency states. If acquisition noncoherently combines the
energy from both states, Eq. (11) uses the total received power. If the receiver
must detect either component independently and each carries a fraction
$\alpha_i$ of the total power, replace $P_t$ by
$\alpha_{\min}P_t$, where
$\alpha_{\min}=\min_i\alpha_i$. For equal components,
$\alpha_{\min}=1/2$, producing a factor-of-two power penalty, or a
$\sqrt{2}$ reduction in range, relative to ideal combined-energy detection.



### Coherent and Noncoherent Integration

Two fundamentally different accumulation models are used in signal detection.

**Coherent integration** assumes that the receiver knows (or has already
estimated) the signal phase and Doppler well enough to align successive
samples before summation. Signal amplitudes are added coherently, producing a
detection statistic proportional to observation time. Under this assumption,

\[
\rho \propto t_{\mathrm{int}},
\]

which leads to

\[
R_{\max}\propto t_{\mathrm{int}}^{1/2}.
\]

This model is appropriate after signal acquisition, when carrier frequency,
phase, and Doppler have already been estimated.

**Noncoherent integration** is required during blind acquisition, where the
receiver does not know the signal phase and therefore combines only measured
energy or power. The signal-to-noise ratio of the energy detector improves
only as

\[
\rho \propto \sqrt{t_{\mathrm{int}}},
\]

which results in

\[
R_{\max}\propto t_{\mathrm{int}}^{1/4}.
\]

Because the beacon considered in this work is detected before its carrier
phase is known, the noncoherent acquisition model is the appropriate one.
Accordingly, all range expressions below are based on noncoherent integration.


## Maximum Communication Distance

At the limiting distance, Eq. (11) is satisfied with equality:

$$P_r = \frac{\rho_{\mathrm{acq}}k_{\mathrm B}T_{\mathrm{sys}}}{\sqrt{t_{\mathrm{int}}}}. \qquad (16)$$

Equations (16)--(22) are derived in this chapter by combining the cited
free-space link equation, aperture relations, and thermal-noise model with the
specified acquisition-energy requirement. They are not quoted from a separate
range formula.

Combining Eqs. (1) and (16) gives the gain form of the range equation:

$$\boxed{R_{\max} = \frac{\lambda}{4\pi}\sqrt{\frac{P_tG_tG_rL\sqrt{t_{\mathrm{int}}}}{\rho_{\mathrm{acq}}k_{\mathrm B}T_{\mathrm{sys}}}}}. \qquad (17)$$

Using effective antenna areas gives

$$\boxed{R_{\max} = \sqrt{\frac{P_tA_{\mathrm{eff},t}A_{\mathrm{eff},r}L\sqrt{t_{\mathrm{int}}}}{\rho_{\mathrm{acq}}k_{\mathrm B}T_{\mathrm{sys}}\lambda^2}}}. \qquad (18)$$

For circular apertures, the result becomes

$$\boxed{R_{\max} = \frac{\pi D_tD_r}{4\lambda}\sqrt{\frac{P_t\eta_t\eta_rL\sqrt{t_{\mathrm{int}}}}{\rho_{\mathrm{acq}}k_{\mathrm B}T_{\mathrm{sys}}}}}. \qquad (19)$$

Equation (19) is the requested relation between transmitter power, antenna
dimensions, receiver temperature, observation time, wavelength, acquisition
requirements, and achievable distance.

It may also be inverted to obtain the required transmitter power:

$$\boxed{P_t = \frac{16\rho_{\mathrm{acq}}k_{\mathrm B}T_{\mathrm{sys}}\lambda^2R^2}{\pi^2\eta_t\eta_rD_t^2D_r^2L\sqrt{t_{\mathrm{int}}}}}. \qquad (20)$$

Similarly, the required product of antenna diameters is

$$\boxed{D_tD_r = \frac{4\lambda R}{\pi}\sqrt{\frac{\rho_{\mathrm{acq}}k_{\mathrm B}T_{\mathrm{sys}}}{P_t\eta_t\eta_rL\sqrt{t_{\mathrm{int}}}}}}. \qquad (21)$$

## Scaling Form

At the neutral-hydrogen frequency
$f_0=1.420405751\ \mathrm{GHz}$, for which
$\lambda\simeq0.2111\ \mathrm{m}$, Eq. (19) can be written as

$$\begin{aligned} R_{\max}\simeq2.05\times \left(P_t=1\ \mathrm{MW}\right)^{1/2}{}\times \left(D_t=10\ \mathrm{m}\right){}\times \left(D_r=10\ \mathrm{m}\right){}\times \left(\eta_t=0.6\right)^{1/2}{}\times \left(\eta_r=0.6\right)^{1/2}{}\times \left(L=1\right)^{1/2} \\ {}\times \left(t_{\mathrm{int}}=1000\ \mathrm{s}\right)^{1/2}{}\times \left(T_{\mathrm{sys}}=30\ \mathrm{K}\right)^{1/2}{}\times \left(\rho_{\mathrm{acq}}=30\right)^{1/2}\mathrm{pc}. \qquad (22) \end{aligned}$$

This numerical example is only a normalization, not an assumed practical
design. For the same parameters, extending the distance to $5\ \mathrm{pc}$
requires approximately $5.9\ \mathrm{MW}$, because the required power grows
as $R^2$.

## Relation to Conventional SETI Range Calculators

Online link-budget tools may give a substantially different range even when
the transmitter power, antenna diameters, frequency, and receiver temperature
are identical to those used above. This does not necessarily indicate a
disagreement in the free-space propagation equation. In particular, the
[SETI Range Calculator](https://www.satsig.net/seticalc.htm) expresses receiver
sensitivity through an analysis bandwidth $B$ and a required carrier-to-noise
ratio $C/N$, whereas the present derivation expresses it through integration
time $t_{\mathrm{int}}$ and required normalized acquisition energy
$\rho_{\mathrm{acq}}$.

### Bandwidth-Based Detection Criterion

For a receiver with system noise temperature $T_{\mathrm{sys}}$, the thermal
noise power in bandwidth $B$ is

$$P_n = k_{\mathrm B}T_{\mathrm{sys}}B. \qquad (23)$$

Equation (23) is the bandwidth-integrated form of the noise-density relation
in Eq. (6) [2, 3]. Equations (24)--(28) then follow algebraically from the
definitions of required $C/N$ and $\rho_{\mathrm{acq}}$.

If the required linear carrier-to-noise ratio is $\Gamma=(C/N)_{\mathrm{req}}$,
the conventional minimum detectable received power is

$$P_{r,\min} = \Gamma k_{\mathrm B}T_{\mathrm{sys}}B. \qquad (24)$$

The calculator substitutes this threshold into the same free-space link
equation used in this chapter. Its range is therefore

$$R_{\max} = \frac{\lambda}{4\pi}\sqrt{\frac{P_tG_tG_rL}{\Gamma k_{\mathrm B}T_{\mathrm{sys}}B}}. \qquad (25)$$

### Energy-Based Acquisition Criterion

The blind-acquisition model instead uses Eq. (16):

$$P_{r,\min} = \frac{\rho_{\mathrm{acq}}k_{\mathrm B}T_{\mathrm{sys}}}{t_{\mathrm{int}}}. \qquad (26)$$

Equations (24) and (26) are equivalent when

$$\boxed{B\Gamma = \frac{\rho_{\mathrm{acq}}}{t_{\mathrm{int}}}}. \qquad (27)$$

Thus, $B\Gamma$ is the bandwidth-based representation of the energy threshold
$\rho_{\mathrm{acq}}/t_{\mathrm{int}}$. A comparison between the two range
calculations is meaningful only when this condition is satisfied. Because
range is inversely proportional to the square root of the detection threshold,
the ratio between the two calculated ranges is

$$\frac{R_{\mathrm{energy}}}{R_{\mathrm{bandwidth}}} = \sqrt{\frac{B\Gamma t_{\mathrm{int}}}{\rho_{\mathrm{acq}}}}. \qquad (28)$$

### Numerical Reconciliation

For the normalized example in Eq. (22),
$t_{\mathrm{int}}=1000\ \mathrm{s}$ and
$\rho_{\mathrm{acq}}=30$. The equivalent bandwidth-based threshold is

$$B\Gamma = \frac{30}{1000\ \mathrm{s}} = 0.03\ \mathrm{Hz}. \qquad (29)$$

Several combinations of bandwidth and required $C/N$ represent exactly the
same receiver sensitivity:

| Bandwidth $B$ | Required $C/N$ | $B\Gamma$ |
| ---: | ---: | ---: |
| $3\ \mathrm{Hz}$ | $-20.00\ \mathrm{dB}$ | $0.03\ \mathrm{Hz}$ |
| $1\ \mathrm{Hz}$ | $-15.23\ \mathrm{dB}$ | $0.03\ \mathrm{Hz}$ |
| $0.001\ \mathrm{Hz}$ | $+14.77\ \mathrm{dB}$ | $0.03\ \mathrm{Hz}$ |

With any of these equivalent combinations and otherwise identical link
parameters, Eq. (25) reproduces the range of approximately
$2.05\ \mathrm{pc}$ obtained from Eq. (22).

For comparison, choosing $B=3\ \mathrm{Hz}$ and a required
$C/N=0.5\ \mathrm{dB}$ gives
$\Gamma=10^{0.5/10}\simeq1.122$ and therefore
$B\Gamma\simeq3.366\ \mathrm{Hz}$. This threshold is approximately 112 times
larger than $0.03\ \mathrm{Hz}$. The corresponding range is smaller by
$\sqrt{112}\simeq10.6$:

$$R_{\max}\simeq\frac{2.05\ \mathrm{pc}}{10.6}\simeq0.194\ \mathrm{pc}. \qquad (30)$$

The difference is therefore a receiver-model difference rather than a
propagation-loss difference.

### Why Integration Time Cannot Be Omitted

The calculator permits negative required $C/N$ values and notes that weak
continuous-wave signals may be detected through long averaging. However, it
does not include integration time as an explicit input and does not define
which observation duration, false-alarm probability, or number of searched
hypotheses corresponds to a selected negative $C/N$. The user must implicitly
encode the processing gain into the required $C/N$ value.

That representation is adequate for a conventional link-budget estimate when
receiver sensitivity has already been specified. It is insufficient for blind
acquisition, where the threshold must also account for the number of frequency,
drift-rate, timing, sky-position, and waveform hypotheses. The parameter
$\rho_{\mathrm{acq}}$ makes these assumptions explicit and connects the
physical link budget to the detection procedure.

### Two-Frequency Power Convention

One further discrepancy can arise from the definition of transmitted power.
The conventional calculator models a single carrier. In this chapter, $P_t$
is the total power of the two-frequency constant-total-power waveform. If both
frequency components are combined in the acquisition statistic, the total
power is appropriate and no correction is required. If each equal-power
component must be detected independently, only $P_t/2$ is available to that
test. The calculated range then becomes

$$R_{\mathrm{single\ component}} = \frac{R_{\mathrm{combined}}}{\sqrt{2}}. \qquad (31)$$

For the normalized example, this changes the range from
$2.05\ \mathrm{pc}$ to approximately $1.45\ \mathrm{pc}$. The power convention
and the acquisition statistic must therefore be stated whenever different
range estimates are compared.

## Interpretation and Design Consequences

Equation (19) gives several immediate results:

1. Range is proportional to each antenna diameter:
   $R_{\max}\propto D_tD_r$. For aperture antennas, doubling either diameter
   doubles the range.
2. Range grows only as the square root of transmitted power and integration
   time:
   $R_{\max}\propto\sqrt{P_t}\,t_{\mathrm{int}}^{1/4}$.
3. Range decreases as the square root of system temperature and required
   acquisition energy:
   $R_{\max}\propto1/\sqrt{T_{\mathrm{sys}}\rho_{\mathrm{acq}}}$.
4. At fixed physical aperture dimensions, range is inversely proportional to
   wavelength, or directly proportional to frequency. This gain is limited in
   practice by surface accuracy, pointing requirements, propagation, and the
   frequency dependence of $T_{\mathrm{sys}}$.
5. A loss factor reduces range only through its square root, but avoiding loss
   before the receiver's first amplifier also prevents a simultaneous increase
   in $T_{\mathrm{sys}}$.
6. Reducing blind-search dimensionality decreases both computation and the
   multiple-trial detection threshold. Waveform design therefore affects range
   even when transmitted power, antennas, and receiver hardware remain
   unchanged.

The free-space result assumes correct pointing, far-field propagation, and a
stationary channel over $t_{\mathrm{int}}$. If phase coherence is lost after a
coherence time $T_c<t_{\mathrm{int}}$, the observation must be divided into
shorter coherent intervals and combined noncoherently. In that case the ideal
linear accumulation of Eq. (11) is no longer attained, and the required
$\rho_{\mathrm{acq}}$ must be increased according to the selected
noncoherent detector. Scintillation, unknown acceleration, oscillator
instability, and unmodelled Doppler drift can all impose such a limit.

Finally, Eqs. (17)--(22) describe acquisition range, not necessarily reliable
data-decoding range. After acquisition, the communication data rate and error
probability introduce a separate $E_b/N_0$ requirement. For a beacon intended
to be found before it is decoded, the acquisition condition is the appropriate
first constraint.
