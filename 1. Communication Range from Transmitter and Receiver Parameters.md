# Communication Range from Transmitter and Receiver Parameters

## Scope

The acquisition strategy developed in *Blind Acquisition* determines how much
signal energy must be collected before a receiver can distinguish a beacon
candidate from thermal noise while searching over an unknown signal state. This
section connects that acquisition requirement to physical transmitter and
receiver parameters. The result is a link equation for the maximum free-space
distance as a function of transmitted power, antenna dimensions, wavelength,
system noise temperature, integration time, propagation losses, and the size of
the blind search.

The derivation applies to a continuously radiated narrowband waveform. It is
therefore more naturally expressed in terms of accumulated signal energy than
in terms of the instantaneous signal-to-noise ratio in an arbitrarily selected
receiver bandwidth.

## Received Power

Let

- $P_t$ be the total RF power delivered to the transmitting antenna;
- $G_t$ and $G_r$ be the transmitter and receiver antenna gains in the
  direction of the link;
- $R$ be the propagation distance;
- $\lambda=c/f_0$ be the wavelength at the nominal carrier frequency;
- $L\leq 1$ be the product of all additional power-transfer factors.

The factor $L$ may include pointing loss, polarization mismatch, atmospheric
or interstellar attenuation, implementation loss, and any other loss not
already included in the antenna gains. In the far field, the Friis equation
gives

$$P_r = P_t G_t G_r \left(\frac{\lambda}{4\pi R}\right)^2 L . \qquad (1)$$

Equation (1) is the standard free-space transmission relation introduced by
Friis [1]. The original paper also gives the equivalent formulation in terms
of the effective areas of the transmitting and receiving antennas.

For aperture antennas,

$$G=\frac{4\pi A_{\mathrm{eff}}}{\lambda^2}. \qquad (2)$$

where $A_{\mathrm{eff}}$ is the effective collecting area. Substitution into
Eq. (1) gives the particularly useful aperture form

$$P_r = \frac{P_t A_{\mathrm{eff},t}A_{\mathrm{eff},r}}{\lambda^2R^2}L . \qquad (3)$$

The gain--effective-area relation in Eq. (2), and its use in free-space link
budgets, are also documented in the NASA/JPL *Propagation Effects Handbook for
Satellite Systems* [2].

For circular aperture antennas of physical diameters $D_t$ and $D_r$,

$$A_{\mathrm{eff},t} = \eta_t\frac{\pi D_t^2}{4}, \qquad A_{\mathrm{eff},r} = \eta_r\frac{\pi D_r^2}{4}. \qquad (4)$$

where $\eta_t$ and $\eta_r$ are aperture efficiencies. Equation (3) then
becomes

$$P_r = \frac{P_t\,\eta_t\eta_r\pi^2D_t^2D_r^2}{16\lambda^2R^2}L . \qquad (5)$$

## Receiver Noise Temperature

The one-sided thermal-noise power spectral density referred to the receiver
input is

$$N_0=k_{\mathrm B}T_{\mathrm{sys}}. \qquad (6)$$

The relation between noise power density and system noise temperature used in
Eq. (6) is the standard $N_0=kT$ representation employed in NASA/JPL
telecommunication link budgets [2, 3]. The numerical value of
$k_{\mathrm B}$ is fixed exactly in the SI and is available from NIST [4].

where $k_{\mathrm B}$ is Boltzmann's constant and $T_{\mathrm{sys}}$ is the
system noise temperature. A useful decomposition is

$$T_{\mathrm{sys}} = T_{\mathrm{sky}} + T_{\mathrm{atm}} + T_{\mathrm{ground}} + T_{\mathrm{spill}} + T_{\mathrm{rx}}. \qquad (7)$$

The receiver contribution can be obtained from its noise figure $F$:

$$T_{\mathrm{rx}}=T_0(F-1). \qquad (8)$$

with $T_0=290\ \mathrm{K}$. Loss ahead of the first low-noise amplifier is
especially expensive. If a passive input network has a linear loss
$\ell\geq1$, physical temperature $T_p$, and is followed by a receiver with
noise temperature $T_{\mathrm{rx}}$, its input-referred contribution is

$$T_{\mathrm{front}} = (\ell-1)T_p+\ell T_{\mathrm{rx}}. \qquad (9)$$

Equations (8) and (9) follow the standard equivalent-input-noise-temperature
relations. NIST gives $T_{\mathrm{rx}}=290(F-1)$ for a two-port receiver [5],
while the passive-loss transformation and the transfer of following receiver
noise through a lossy input network are derived in the JPL antenna-system
noise-temperature treatment [6].

Thus, increasing antenna area, reducing preamplifier loss, and reducing system
temperature enter the range equation on an equal physical footing.

## Energy Required for Acquisition

Let $B$ be the noise-equivalent bandwidth of one energy-detector cell and let $t_{\mathrm{int}}$ be the total noncoherent integration time assigned to one tested signal hypothesis. The mean thermal-noise power in one detector cell is

$$P_n=k_{\mathrm B}T_{\mathrm{sys}}B. \qquad (10)$$

For an ideal total-power radiometer observing stationary Gaussian noise, the rms fluctuation of the measured noise power after averaging for $t_{\mathrm{int}}$ is

$$\sigma_P=\frac{k_{\mathrm B}T_{\mathrm{sys}}B}{\sqrt{Bt_{\mathrm{int}}}}=k_{\mathrm B}T_{\mathrm{sys}}\sqrt{\frac{B}{t_{\mathrm{int}}}}. \qquad (11)$$

Define the normalized noncoherent acquisition threshold as

$$\rho_{\mathrm{acq}}\equiv\frac{P_r}{\sigma_P}. \qquad (12)$$

The minimum detectable received power is therefore

$$P_{r,\min}=\rho_{\mathrm{acq}}k_{\mathrm B}T_{\mathrm{sys}}\sqrt{\frac{B}{t_{\mathrm{int}}}}. \qquad (13)$$

The value of $\rho_{\mathrm{acq}}$ is not universal. It depends on the selected energy statistic, the required probability of detection, the allowed probability of false alarm, and the number of independent hypotheses tested during blind acquisition.

If $M$ statistically independent cells are searched and the allowed experiment-wide false-alarm probability is $P_{\mathrm{FA}}$, the required single-cell false-alarm probability is

$$p_{\mathrm{fa}}=1-\left(1-P_{\mathrm{FA}}\right)^{1/M}\simeq\frac{P_{\mathrm{FA}}}{M},\qquad P_{\mathrm{FA}}\ll1. \qquad (14)$$

If the searched frequency interval is $W_f$, the number of independent frequency cells is approximately

$$M_f\simeq\frac{W_f}{B}. \qquad (15)$$

Searches over frequency drift, symbol period, time offset, sky position, polarization, or waveform family multiply the total number of hypotheses. Reducing blind-search dimensionality therefore decreases both computational cost and the acquisition threshold.

For a two-frequency constant-total-power waveform, $P_t$ denotes the combined power in both frequency states. If the acquisition statistic combines the energy from both states, the total received power is used in Eq. (13). If each component must be detected independently and component $i$ carries a fraction $\alpha_i$ of the total power, replace $P_t$ by $\alpha_{\min}P_t$, where $\alpha_{\min}=\min_i\alpha_i$. For equal components, $\alpha_{\min}=1/2$.

### Coherent and Noncoherent Integration

Coherent and noncoherent integration use different detection statistics and therefore produce different time scalings.

In coherent integration, the receiver compensates the carrier frequency, Doppler evolution, and phase before summing complex voltage samples. At a fixed coherent-detection threshold, the minimum detectable received power scales as $t_{\mathrm{int}}^{-1}$, and the free-space range scales as

$$R_{\max,\mathrm{coh}}\propto t_{\mathrm{int}}^{1/2}. \qquad (16)$$

In noncoherent integration, the carrier phase is not recovered. The receiver forms an energy or power statistic and averages independent power estimates. Equation (11) shows that the rms noise fluctuation decreases as $t_{\mathrm{int}}^{-1/2}$. Consequently, the minimum detectable received power scales as $t_{\mathrm{int}}^{-1/2}$, and the free-space range scales as $t_{\mathrm{int}}^{1/4}$ [7].

The present chapter concerns blind energy acquisition without phase recovery. The noncoherent model is therefore used below.

## Maximum Communication Distance

At the limiting distance, the received power from Eq. (5) equals the noncoherent threshold from Eq. (13). Combining Eqs. (1) and (13) gives the gain form of the range equation:

$$\boxed{R_{\max}=\frac{\lambda}{4\pi}\sqrt{\frac{P_tG_tG_rL}{\rho_{\mathrm{acq}}k_{\mathrm B}T_{\mathrm{sys}}}}\left(\frac{t_{\mathrm{int}}}{B}\right)^{1/4}}. \qquad (17)$$

Using effective antenna areas gives

$$\boxed{R_{\max}=\sqrt{\frac{P_tA_{\mathrm{eff},t}A_{\mathrm{eff},r}L}{\rho_{\mathrm{acq}}k_{\mathrm B}T_{\mathrm{sys}}\lambda^2}}\left(\frac{t_{\mathrm{int}}}{B}\right)^{1/4}}. \qquad (18)$$

For circular apertures, the result becomes

$$\boxed{R_{\max}=\frac{\pi D_tD_r}{4\lambda}\sqrt{\frac{P_t\eta_t\eta_rL}{\rho_{\mathrm{acq}}k_{\mathrm B}T_{\mathrm{sys}}}}\left(\frac{t_{\mathrm{int}}}{B}\right)^{1/4}}. \qquad (19)$$

Equation (19) is the required relation between transmitter power, antenna dimensions, receiver temperature, detector bandwidth, noncoherent integration time, wavelength, acquisition threshold, and achievable distance.

It may be inverted to obtain the required transmitter power:

$$\boxed{P_t=\frac{16\rho_{\mathrm{acq}}k_{\mathrm B}T_{\mathrm{sys}}\lambda^2R^2}{\pi^2\eta_t\eta_rD_t^2D_r^2L}\sqrt{\frac{B}{t_{\mathrm{int}}}}}. \qquad (20)$$

Similarly, the required product of antenna diameters is

$$\boxed{D_tD_r=\frac{4\lambda R}{\pi}\sqrt{\frac{\rho_{\mathrm{acq}}k_{\mathrm B}T_{\mathrm{sys}}}{P_t\eta_t\eta_rL}}\left(\frac{B}{t_{\mathrm{int}}}\right)^{1/4}}. \qquad (21)$$

## Relation to Conventional SETI Range Calculators

A conventional bandwidth-based link-budget calculator [9] expresses receiver sensitivity through an analysis bandwidth $B$ and a required linear carrier-to-noise ratio $\Gamma=(C/N)_{\mathrm{req}}$. The corresponding minimum detectable received power is

$$P_{r,\min}=\Gamma k_{\mathrm B}T_{\mathrm{sys}}B. \qquad (22)$$

The noncoherent radiometer model instead uses Eq. (13). The two descriptions are equivalent when

$$\boxed{\Gamma B=\rho_{\mathrm{acq}}\sqrt{\frac{B}{t_{\mathrm{int}}}}}. \qquad (23)$$

Equivalently,

$$\boxed{\Gamma=\frac{\rho_{\mathrm{acq}}}{\sqrt{Bt_{\mathrm{int}}}}}. \qquad (24)$$

Thus, a negative required $C/N$ can represent noncoherent processing gain, but only when the detector bandwidth and integration time corresponding to that value are specified.

For the normalization in Eq. (19), $t_{\mathrm{int}}=1000\ \mathrm{s}$, $B=1\ \mathrm{Hz}$, and $\rho_{\mathrm{acq}}=30$. Equation (24) gives

$$\Gamma=\frac{30}{\sqrt{1000}}\simeq0.9487,\qquad(C/N)_{\mathrm{req}}\simeq-0.23\ \mathrm{dB}. \qquad (25)$$

A comparison between different range calculators is meaningful only when they use equivalent values of $B$, $t_{\mathrm{int}}$, detection threshold, and false-alarm criterion.

### Why Integration Time and Bandwidth Cannot Be Omitted

A required $C/N$ value by itself does not define receiver sensitivity for a blind energy search. The same numerical $C/N$ may correspond to different observation times, detector bandwidths, false-alarm probabilities, and numbers of searched hypotheses.

Likewise, integration time alone is insufficient in a noncoherent model. Equation (11) depends on the time-bandwidth product $Bt_{\mathrm{int}}$, while Eq. (19) depends on $(t_{\mathrm{int}}/B)^{1/4}$. Both parameters must therefore be specified explicitly.

The total Doppler search interval is not the detector bandwidth $B$. If the receiver searches a frequency interval $W_f$, it divides that interval into approximately $W_f/B$ independent detector cells. For example, a Doppler interval of approximately $950\ \mathrm{kHz}$ searched with $B=1\ \mathrm{Hz}$ contains approximately $950\,000$ frequency cells.

### Two-Frequency Power Convention

In this chapter, $P_t$ is the total power of the two-frequency constant-total-power waveform. If the acquisition statistic combines both frequency states, no correction is required. If each equal-power component is tested independently, only $P_t/2$ is available to that test. From Eq. (19),

$$R_{\mathrm{single\ component}}=\frac{R_{\mathrm{combined}}}{\sqrt{2}}. \qquad (26)$$

The power convention and the acquisition statistic must therefore be stated whenever range estimates are compared.

## Interpretation and Design Consequences

Equation (19) gives several immediate results:

1. Range is proportional to each antenna diameter: $R_{\max}\propto D_tD_r$.
2. Range grows as the square root of transmitted power: $R_{\max}\propto P_t^{1/2}$.
3. For noncoherent integration with fixed detector bandwidth, range grows only as the fourth root of integration time: $R_{\max}\propto t_{\mathrm{int}}^{1/4}$.
4. Range decreases as the fourth root of detector bandwidth: $R_{\max}\propto B^{-1/4}$.
5. Range decreases as the square root of system temperature and acquisition threshold: $R_{\max}\propto(T_{\mathrm{sys}}\rho_{\mathrm{acq}})^{-1/2}$.
6. At fixed physical aperture dimensions, range is inversely proportional to wavelength, or directly proportional to frequency.
7. The power-transfer factor changes range through $L^{1/2}$.
8. Reducing blind-search dimensionality decreases both computational cost and the multiple-trial detection threshold.

The ideal radiometer equation assumes stationary Gaussian noise, statistically independent power samples, stable receiver gain, and a signal that remains within one detector cell. Scintillation, oscillator instability, unmodelled Doppler drift, spectral leakage, windowing loss, gain fluctuations, and radio-frequency interference can reduce the effective number of independent samples or require a larger value of $\rho_{\mathrm{acq}}$.

Finally, Eqs. (17)--(26) describe acquisition range, not necessarily reliable data-decoding range. After acquisition, carrier tracking and data decoding introduce separate requirements.
