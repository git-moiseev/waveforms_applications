# Communication Range from Transmitter and Receiver Parameters

## Scope

The acquisition strategy developed in *Blind Acquisition* determines how much
signal energy must be collected before a receiver can distinguish a beacon
candidate from thermal noise while searching over an unknown signal state. This
section connects that acquisition requirement to physical transmitter and
receiver parameters. The result is a link equation for the maximum free-space
distance as a function of transmitted power, antenna dimensions, wavelength,
system noise temperature, integration time, propagation losses, and the size of
the blind search.

The derivation applies to a continuously radiated narrowband waveform
detected by a blind energy search without carrier-phase recovery. It is
therefore expressed in terms of the excess power measured in a finite
noise-equivalent bandwidth and averaged over a finite observation time.

## Received Power

Let

- $P_t$ be the total RF power delivered to the transmitting antenna;
- $G_t$ and $G_r$ be the transmitter and receiver antenna gains in the
  direction of the link;
- $R$ be the propagation distance;
- $\lambda=c/f_0$ be the wavelength at the nominal carrier frequency;
- $L\leq 1$ be the product of all additional power-transfer factors.

The factor $L$ may include pointing loss, polarization mismatch, atmospheric
or interstellar attenuation, implementation loss, and any other loss not
already included in the antenna gains. In the far field, the Friis equation
gives

$$
P_r = P_t G_t G_r \left(\frac{\lambda}{4\pi R}\right)^2 L . \qquad (1)
$$

Equation (1) is the standard free-space transmission relation introduced by
Friis [1]. The original paper also gives the equivalent formulation in terms
of the effective areas of the transmitting and receiving antennas.

For aperture antennas,

$$
G=\frac{4\pi A_{\mathrm{eff}}}{\lambda^2}. \qquad (2)
$$

where $A_{\mathrm{eff}}$ is the effective collecting area. Substitution into
Eq. (1) gives the particularly useful aperture form

$$
P_r = \frac{P_t A_{\mathrm{eff},t}A_{\mathrm{eff},r}}{\lambda^2R^2}L . \qquad (3)
$$

The gain--effective-area relation in Eq. (2), and its use in free-space link
budgets, are also documented in the NASA/JPL *Propagation Effects Handbook for
Satellite Systems* [2].

For circular aperture antennas of physical diameters $D_t$ and $D_r$,

$$
A_{\mathrm{eff},t} = \eta_t\frac{\pi D_t^2}{4}, \qquad A_{\mathrm{eff},r} = \eta_r\frac{\pi D_r^2}{4}. \qquad (4)
$$

where $\eta_t$ and $\eta_r$ are aperture efficiencies. Equation (3) then
becomes

$$
P_r = \frac{P_t\,\eta_t\eta_r\pi^2D_t^2D_r^2}{16\lambda^2R^2}L . \qquad (5)
$$

## Receiver Noise Temperature

The one-sided thermal-noise power spectral density referred to the receiver
input is

$$
N_0=k_{\mathrm B}T_{\mathrm{sys}}. \qquad (6)
$$

The relation between noise power density and system noise temperature used in
Eq. (6) is the standard $N_0=kT$ representation employed in NASA/JPL
telecommunication link budgets [2, 3]. The numerical value of
$k_{\mathrm B}$ is fixed exactly in the SI and is available from NIST [4].

where $k_{\mathrm B}$ is Boltzmann's constant and $T_{\mathrm{sys}}$ is the
system noise temperature. A useful decomposition is

$$
T_{\mathrm{sys}} = T_{\mathrm{sky}} + T_{\mathrm{atm}} + T_{\mathrm{ground}} + T_{\mathrm{spill}} + T_{\mathrm{rx}}. \qquad (7)
$$

The receiver contribution can be obtained from its noise figure $F$:

$$
T_{\mathrm{rx}}=T_0(F-1). \qquad (8)
$$

with $T_0=290\ \mathrm{K}$. Loss ahead of the first low-noise amplifier is
especially expensive. If a passive input network has a linear loss
$\ell\geq1$, physical temperature $T_p$, and is followed by a receiver with
noise temperature $T_{\mathrm{rx}}$, its input-referred contribution is

$$
T_{\mathrm{front}} = (\ell-1)T_p+\ell T_{\mathrm{rx}}. \qquad (9)
$$

Equations (8) and (9) follow the standard equivalent-input-noise-temperature
relations. NIST gives $T_{\mathrm{rx}}=290(F-1)$ for a two-port receiver [5],
while the passive-loss transformation and the transfer of following receiver
noise through a lossy input network are derived in the JPL antenna-system
noise-temperature treatment [6].

Thus, increasing antenna area, reducing preamplifier loss, and reducing system
temperature enter the range equation on an equal physical footing.

## Energy Required for Acquisition

Let $B$ be the noise-equivalent bandwidth of one energy-detector cell and let
$t_{\mathrm{int}}$ be the total noncoherent integration time assigned to one
tested signal hypothesis. The receiver measures power in the selected
time--frequency cell and compares it with the expected thermal-noise power

$$
P_n=k_{\mathrm B}T_{\mathrm{sys}}B. \qquad (10)
$$

For an ideal total-power radiometer observing stationary Gaussian noise, the
rms fluctuation of the measured noise power after averaging for
$t_{\mathrm{int}}$ is

$$
\sigma_P =
\frac{k_{\mathrm B}T_{\mathrm{sys}}B}
{\sqrt{B t_{\mathrm{int}}}}
=
k_{\mathrm B}T_{\mathrm{sys}}
\sqrt{\frac{B}{t_{\mathrm{int}}}}. \qquad (11)
$$

Equation (11) is the radiometer equation written in power units for one
polarization and unit detection efficiency. Practical losses, windowing,
overlap, unequal weighting, multiple polarizations, or correlated samples can
be represented by an additional detector-efficiency factor or by replacing
$B t_{\mathrm{int}}$ with the effective number of independent noise samples.

Define the normalized noncoherent acquisition threshold as

$$
\rho_{\mathrm{acq}}
\equiv
\frac{P_r}{\sigma_P}. \qquad (12)
$$

The minimum detectable received power is therefore

$$
\boxed{
P_{r,\min}
=
\rho_{\mathrm{acq}}k_{\mathrm B}T_{\mathrm{sys}}
\sqrt{\frac{B}{t_{\mathrm{int}}}}
}.
\qquad (13)
$$

The value of $\rho_{\mathrm{acq}}$ is not universal. It is determined by the
chosen energy statistic, the required probability of detection, the allowed
probability of false alarm, and the number of independent hypotheses tested
during blind acquisition.

If $M$ statistically independent cells are searched and the allowed
experiment-wide false-alarm probability is $P_{\mathrm{FA}}$, the required
single-cell false-alarm probability is

$$
p_{\mathrm{fa}}
=
1-\left(1-P_{\mathrm{FA}}\right)^{1/M}
\simeq
\frac{P_{\mathrm{FA}}}{M},
\qquad P_{\mathrm{FA}}\ll1. \qquad (14)
$$

For a normalized exponential single-cell energy statistic, the noise-only
threshold is

$$
\gamma
=
-\ln p_{\mathrm{fa}}
\simeq
\ln\left(\frac{M}{P_{\mathrm{FA}}}\right). \qquad (15)
$$

For an average of many independent power samples, the exact noise-only and
signal-plus-noise distributions are gamma and noncentral chi-square
distributions, respectively. Consequently, the exact value of
$\rho_{\mathrm{acq}}$ must be obtained from the selected statistic and target
probability of detection. Equations (14) and (15) show only the
multiple-trial scaling of the threshold.

If the searched frequency interval is $W_f$, the number of independent
frequency cells is approximately

$$
M_f\simeq\frac{W_f}{B}. \qquad (16)
$$

Searches over frequency drift, symbol period, time offset, sky position,
polarization, or waveform family multiply the total number of hypotheses.
Reducing blind-search dimensionality therefore decreases both computational
cost and the acquisition threshold.

For a two-frequency constant-total-power waveform, $P_t$ denotes the combined
power in both frequency states. If the acquisition statistic combines the
energy from both states, the total received power is used in Eq. (13). If each
component must be detected independently and component $i$ carries a fraction
$\alpha_i$ of the total power, replace $P_t$ by
$\alpha_{\min}P_t$, where
$\alpha_{\min}=\min_i\alpha_i$. For equal components,
$\alpha_{\min}=1/2$.

### Coherent and Noncoherent Integration

Coherent and noncoherent integration use different sufficient statistics and
therefore produce different time scalings.

In **coherent integration**, the receiver compensates the carrier frequency,
Doppler evolution, and phase before summing complex voltage samples. The
received signal energy grows linearly with observation time, and the coherent
detection statistic can be written in terms of

$$
\frac{E_s}{N_0}
=
\frac{P_rt_{\mathrm{int}}}
{k_{\mathrm B}T_{\mathrm{sys}}}. \qquad (17)
$$

At a fixed coherent-detection threshold,
$P_{r,\min}\propto t_{\mathrm{int}}^{-1}$.
Because free-space received power decreases as $R^{-2}$, coherent acquisition
would give

$$
R_{\max}\propto t_{\mathrm{int}}^{1/2}. \qquad (18)
$$

In **noncoherent integration**, the carrier phase is not recovered. The
receiver first forms an energy or power statistic and then averages independent
power estimates. The rms fluctuation of that estimate decreases only as
$(B t_{\mathrm{int}})^{-1/2}$, as shown by Eq. (11). Therefore,

$$
P_{r,\min}\propto t_{\mathrm{int}}^{-1/2},
\qquad
R_{\max}\propto t_{\mathrm{int}}^{1/4}. \qquad (19)
$$

The present chapter concerns blind energy acquisition without phase recovery,
so Eqs. (11)--(16) and the noncoherent scaling in Eq. (19) are used below.

The bandwidth $B$ cannot be omitted from a dimensionally complete
noncoherent range equation. It may equivalently be represented by a coherent
segment duration $T_c$ when $B\simeq1/T_c$. In that representation, the total
observation contains approximately
$N=t_{\mathrm{int}}/T_c\simeq B t_{\mathrm{int}}$
independent power samples.

## Maximum Communication Distance

At the limiting distance, the received power from Eq. (1) equals the
noncoherent threshold in Eq. (13):

$$
P_r
=
\rho_{\mathrm{acq}}k_{\mathrm B}T_{\mathrm{sys}}
\sqrt{\frac{B}{t_{\mathrm{int}}}}. \qquad (20)
$$

Combining Eqs. (1) and (20) gives the gain form of the range equation:

$$
\boxed{
R_{\max}
=
\frac{\lambda}{4\pi}
\sqrt{
\frac{P_tG_tG_rL}
{\rho_{\mathrm{acq}}k_{\mathrm B}T_{\mathrm{sys}}}
}
\left(\frac{t_{\mathrm{int}}}{B}\right)^{1/4}
}.
\qquad (21)
$$

Using effective antenna areas gives

$$
\boxed{
R_{\max}
=
\sqrt{
\frac{
P_tA_{\mathrm{eff},t}A_{\mathrm{eff},r}L
}{
\rho_{\mathrm{acq}}k_{\mathrm B}T_{\mathrm{sys}}\lambda^2
}
}
\left(\frac{t_{\mathrm{int}}}{B}\right)^{1/4}
}.
\qquad (22)
$$

For circular apertures, the result becomes

$$
\boxed{
R_{\max}
=
\frac{\pi D_tD_r}{4\lambda}
\sqrt{
\frac{
P_t\eta_t\eta_rL
}{
\rho_{\mathrm{acq}}k_{\mathrm B}T_{\mathrm{sys}}
}
}
\left(\frac{t_{\mathrm{int}}}{B}\right)^{1/4}
}.
\qquad (23)
$$

Equation (23) is the required relation for a blind, noncoherent energy search.
It differs from the coherent result by replacing
$t_{\mathrm{int}}^{1/2}$ with
$(t_{\mathrm{int}}/B)^{1/4}$.

Solving Eq. (23) for transmitter power gives

$$
\boxed{
P_t
=
\frac{
16\rho_{\mathrm{acq}}k_{\mathrm B}T_{\mathrm{sys}}
\lambda^2R^2
}{
\pi^2\eta_t\eta_rD_t^2D_r^2L
}
\sqrt{\frac{B}{t_{\mathrm{int}}}}
}.
\qquad (24)
$$

Similarly, the required product of antenna diameters is

$$
\boxed{
D_tD_r
=
\frac{4\lambda R}{\pi}
\sqrt{
\frac{
\rho_{\mathrm{acq}}k_{\mathrm B}T_{\mathrm{sys}}
}{
P_t\eta_t\eta_rL
}
}
\left(\frac{B}{t_{\mathrm{int}}}\right)^{1/4}
}.
\qquad (25)
$$

## Scaling Form

At the neutral-hydrogen frequency
$f_0=1.420405751\ \mathrm{GHz}$, for which
$\lambda\simeq0.2111\ \mathrm{m}$, Eq. (23) can be written as

$$
\begin{aligned}
R_{\max}\simeq
0.365\ \mathrm{pc}
&\left(\frac{P_t}{1\ \mathrm{MW}}\right)^{1/2}
\left(\frac{D_t}{10\ \mathrm{m}}\right)
\left(\frac{D_r}{10\ \mathrm{m}}\right)
\left(\frac{\eta_t}{0.6}\right)^{1/2}
\left(\frac{\eta_r}{0.6}\right)^{1/2} \\
&\times
\left(\frac{L}{1}\right)^{1/2}
\left(\frac{t_{\mathrm{int}}}{1000\ \mathrm{s}}\right)^{1/4}
\left(\frac{B}{1\ \mathrm{Hz}}\right)^{-1/4}
\left(\frac{T_{\mathrm{sys}}}{30\ \mathrm{K}}\right)^{-1/2}
\left(\frac{\rho_{\mathrm{acq}}}{30}\right)^{-1/2}.
\qquad (26)
\end{aligned}
$$

This numerical example is only a normalization. The bandwidth value
$B=1\ \mathrm{Hz}$ is stated explicitly because a noncoherent range cannot be
calculated from integration time alone. With the same parameters, extending
the range to $5\ \mathrm{pc}$ requires approximately

$$
P_t
\simeq
1\ \mathrm{MW}
\left(\frac{5}{0.365}\right)^2
\simeq
188\ \mathrm{MW}. \qquad (27)
$$

## Relation to Conventional SETI Range Calculators

A conventional bandwidth-based calculator specifies the receiver threshold
through an analysis bandwidth $B$ and a required carrier-to-noise ratio
$\Gamma=(C/N)_{\mathrm{req}}$. Its minimum detectable received power is

$$
P_{r,\min}
=
\Gamma k_{\mathrm B}T_{\mathrm{sys}}B. \qquad (28)
$$

The noncoherent radiometer model instead uses Eq. (13). The two descriptions
are equivalent when

$$
\boxed{
\Gamma B
=
\rho_{\mathrm{acq}}
\sqrt{\frac{B}{t_{\mathrm{int}}}}
}
\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad
\boxed{
\Gamma
=
\frac{\rho_{\mathrm{acq}}}
{\sqrt{B t_{\mathrm{int}}}}
}.
\qquad (29)
$$

Thus, a negative required $C/N$ can represent noncoherent processing gain, but
only when the bandwidth and integration time corresponding to that value are
known.

The ratio between a noncoherent range calculated from Eq. (23) and a
bandwidth-based range calculated from Eq. (28) is

$$
\boxed{
\frac{R_{\mathrm{noncoherent}}}
{R_{\mathrm{bandwidth}}}
=
\sqrt{
\frac{
\Gamma B
}{
\rho_{\mathrm{acq}}\sqrt{B/t_{\mathrm{int}}}
}
}
}.
\qquad (30)
$$

For the normalization in Eq. (26),
$t_{\mathrm{int}}=1000\ \mathrm{s}$,
$B=1\ \mathrm{Hz}$, and
$\rho_{\mathrm{acq}}=30$. Equation (29) gives

$$
\Gamma
=
\frac{30}{\sqrt{1000}}
\simeq0.9487,
\qquad
(C/N)_{\mathrm{req}}
\simeq-0.23\ \mathrm{dB}. \qquad (31)
$$

For example, if a conventional calculator instead uses
$B=3\ \mathrm{Hz}$ and
$(C/N)_{\mathrm{req}}=0.5\ \mathrm{dB}$, then
$\Gamma\simeq1.122$ and
$\Gamma B\simeq3.366\ \mathrm{Hz}$. The equivalent noncoherent threshold for
$t_{\mathrm{int}}=1000\ \mathrm{s}$,
$B=3\ \mathrm{Hz}$, and
$\rho_{\mathrm{acq}}=30$ is

$$
\rho_{\mathrm{acq}}\sqrt{\frac{B}{t_{\mathrm{int}}}}
=
30\sqrt{\frac{3}{1000}}
\simeq1.643\ \mathrm{Hz}.
$$

The bandwidth-based threshold is therefore approximately $2.05$ times larger,
and its calculated range is smaller by a factor of
$\sqrt{2.05}\simeq1.43$. The discrepancy is a receiver-model difference, not
a propagation-loss difference.

### Why Integration Time and Bandwidth Cannot Be Omitted

A required $C/N$ value by itself does not define receiver sensitivity for a
blind energy search. The same numerical $C/N$ may correspond to different
observation times, detector bandwidths, false-alarm probabilities, and numbers
of searched hypotheses.

Likewise, integration time alone is insufficient in a noncoherent model.
Equation (11) depends on the time--bandwidth product
$B t_{\mathrm{int}}$, while the range equation depends on
$(t_{\mathrm{int}}/B)^{1/4}$. Both $B$ and $t_{\mathrm{int}}$ must therefore be
specified explicitly.

### Two-Frequency Power Convention

The conventional calculator models a single carrier. In this chapter, $P_t$
is the total power of the two-frequency constant-total-power waveform. If the
acquisition statistic combines both frequency states, no correction is
required. If each equal-power component is tested independently, only $P_t/2$
is available to that test. From Eq. (23),

$$
R_{\mathrm{single\ component}}
=
\frac{R_{\mathrm{combined}}}{\sqrt{2}}.
$$

The power convention and the acquisition statistic must therefore be stated
whenever range estimates are compared.

## Interpretation and Design Consequences

Equation (23) gives several immediate results:

1. Range is proportional to each antenna diameter:
   $R_{\max}\propto D_tD_r$.
2. Range grows as the square root of transmitted power:
   $R_{\max}\propto P_t^{1/2}$.
3. For noncoherent integration, range grows only as the fourth root of
   integration time:
   $R_{\max}\propto t_{\mathrm{int}}^{1/4}$.
4. Range decreases as the fourth root of detector bandwidth:
   $R_{\max}\propto B^{-1/4}$.
5. Range decreases as the square root of system temperature and normalized
   acquisition threshold:
   $R_{\max}\propto
   (T_{\mathrm{sys}}\rho_{\mathrm{acq}})^{-1/2}$.
6. At fixed physical aperture dimensions, range is inversely proportional to
   wavelength, or directly proportional to frequency.
7. A power-transfer factor $L$ changes range through $L^{1/2}$.
8. Reducing blind-search dimensionality decreases both computation and the
   multiple-trial threshold.

The ideal radiometer equation assumes stationary Gaussian noise, statistically
independent power samples, stable gain, and a stationary signal within one
detector cell. Scintillation, oscillator instability, unmodelled Doppler drift,
frequency leakage, windowing loss, gain fluctuations, and radio-frequency
interference can reduce the effective number of independent samples or require
a larger $\rho_{\mathrm{acq}}$.

Finally, Eqs. (21)--(31) describe acquisition range, not necessarily reliable
data-decoding range. After acquisition, carrier tracking and data decoding
introduce separate requirements.
